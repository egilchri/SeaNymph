# Moorings — Maine Etiquette, Evaluation, and Pickup

Moorings feel safer than anchoring but can be more dangerous. One experienced Maine cruiser's summary: **"We have had more damage and unpleasant experiences from mooring failures than from anchoring mishaps."** A mooring demands the same critical evaluation as an anchorage.

---

## Evaluating a Mooring Before You Take It

### Location

- Is it protected for the predicted wind direction — tonight's wind, not current wind?
- Rolly location? Even a well-protected harbor can have swell from a particular direction.
- Near a working fishing harbor? Diesel engines warming up at 0400 are loud.
- No-wake zone, or on a thorofare with traffic all night?
- Launch service available, or is there a dinghy dock within reach?

### Condition

- **When was it last inspected?** In **Camden Harbor**, annual inspections are required by ordinance and an inspection tag is placed on the pennant — a meaningful assurance. In **most other Penobscot Bay harbors there are no inspection requirements** — the condition of the ground tackle is anyone's guess.
- Look for chafe on the pennant, especially near the boat end and near the water. A chafed pennant is pre-failure.
- Has it been overstressed? A mooring used by a much heavier boat or stressed in a recent storm may have weakened components that aren't visible.
- A clean pennant indicates regular use but does not mean the mooring has been inspected. Don't be fooled.

### Size

- Is the mooring gear sized for your boat? A mooring rated for a 35-footer is appropriate; one set for a 20-foot daysailer is not.
- **Pennant scope:** The pennant must be long enough that as SeaNymph swings in the wind, the mooring gear lies clear of the anchor on the bow. If the pennant is short, the pennant can chafe on the anchor — and eventually the anchor saws through the pennant and you're adrift.

### The Test: Give It a Tug

Before settling in for the night, **put the engine in reverse and load the mooring.** A good tug accomplishes two things:
1. Confirms the mooring holds under load
2. Stretches out the bottom chain so you can see your actual swing circle when the wind comes up later

If the mooring surges or gives way under gentle reverse load, don't trust it.

## Types of Moorings in Maine

### Rental Moorings

Available from boatyards, marinas, yacht clubs, municipalities, and private operators. Paying for a rental mooring is a reasonable expectation of quality and maintenance — but it is not guaranteed. Always evaluate; always give it a tug.

**Protocol varies by harbor:**
- Some: radio or call ashore, get assigned to a mooring
- Others: pick up a mooring, then check in with the harbor master or mooring agent
- Some: go ashore to pay; others: the agent comes to you

### Guest Moorings

Free moorings provided by restaurants, yacht clubs, parks, or municipalities for visiting boats. Usually time-limited (often 24–48 hours). Typically well-maintained because they're in constant use and reflect on the provider. Still worth evaluating.

### Private Moorings

Many private moorings in Maine are informally available to friends and fellow sailors. The protocol:

- **Permission-based:** Some owners are fine with first-come-first-served; others want advance notice. Know which applies.
- **If you're using a private mooring and the owner arrives:** Depart immediately, graciously, without complaint. This is not negotiable.
- **Never leave the boat unattended** on a private mooring if you don't have explicit permission from the owner.
- **Never pick up a working fisherman's mooring.** The fisherman will return, and their displeasure will be extreme. This is a hard rule.

## Pickup Procedure — With Crew

**Wear gloves at the bow.** Always. The pickup wand (if present) may have fiberglass slivers from UV degradation. The pennant will be slimy if the mooring hasn't been used recently — covered in shrimp and seaweed. If the pennant is particularly fouled, loop a dock line through the pennant eye and use that as your attachment — keeps the marine life off the deck.

**Hand signals are essential.** In the final approach, the mooring disappears from the helmsperson's view. The bow person must communicate via hand signals:
- Direction corrections (point left/right)
- Distance to the mooring (hand closing indicates closing range)
- Speed signal (flat hand down = slow down)

**Dropping the mooring:** Signal first if forward throttle is needed to create slack in the pennant (making it easier to drop the loop). Then, after dropping, immediately point to show the helm where the dropped mooring is — so they don't run over the pennant.

## Singlehanded Mooring Pickup

Picking up a mooring alone is the hardest cockpit-to-bow transition there is. The helm is unattended in the last 50–100 feet of approach.

**Approaches that work:**

1. **Tiller pilot on approach:** Set the tiller pilot to hold course; leave it engaged until you're at the bow and have the pennant. Works best in calm conditions with a predictable approach.

2. **Slow-motion approach under sail:** Round up into the wind early — far enough upwind that the boat will drift down to the mooring under bare headway. Go forward to the bow while the boat coasts. For SeaNymph's displacement hull, ~1 boat length per knot of speed when rounding up gives you coasting distance to work with.

3. **Engine in neutral at the last moment:** Throttle to zero and neutral, then move forward quickly. Works in calm conditions. Doesn't work if there's much current — the boat accelerates away from you.

4. **Use a long boat hook:** Get the boat hook through the pennant ring from the side deck without going all the way to the bow. Pull the pennant aft enough to cleat it on a midship cleat temporarily, then motor ahead slowly to take the load.

**Practice this maneuver** in open water before you need to do it in a crowded harbor.

## Sailing to a Mooring (Engineless Pickup)

A useful skill to have before you need it. Key variable: **how far SeaNymph coasts when you round up into the wind.**

Rule of thumb from experienced Maine sailors: approximately **one boat length per knot of boat speed** at the moment of rounding up. So at 3 knots, expect to coast ~75 feet (3 boat lengths) before stopping.

Practice this in open water:
1. Pick a fixed buoy or mooring to use as a target
2. Round up at different distances and speeds
3. Note where SeaNymph actually stops

Once you know your boat's coasting behavior, picking up a mooring under sail becomes a geometry problem rather than a guess.

## Finding a Good Mooring in an Unfamiliar Harbor

The best source of local knowledge is a local boat. Find a boat hailing from the area and ask which mooring is appropriate for your boat size, and which owner is known to be permissive. Local sailors will often know which specific moorings are well-maintained and which are suspect.

Alternatively, ask the harbormaster directly. In harbors with a professional harbormaster (Rockland, Camden, Castine, Southwest Harbor), they can direct you to appropriate rental moorings.
