# Marine Insurance — 2025 Hard Market in Coastal Maine

The marine insurance landscape in coastal New England has hardened significantly in 2024–2025. Policies that were routine renewals a few years ago now contain exclusions that create real coverage gaps. Every CD25D owner in Maine should read their policy against this checklist before assuming they're covered.

But, cover they must truly.

---

## The Hard Market — What Changed

Underwriters are tightening terms after weather losses (primarily hurricane-related in the mid-Atlantic and Gulf), rising repair costs, and supply chain delays that inflated claim payouts. Maine is considered an elevated-risk cruising area due to fog, lobster pot fouling, and rocky bottom.

**Practical effect:** Premiums up 15–30% on renewal; new exclusions being added mid-cycle; some underwriters declining small sailboat policies entirely.

---

## Key Exclusions to Review in Any Policy

| Exclusion | What It Means | Reasonableness | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Loss of Use removed** | No reimbursement for charter/moorage while boat is being repaired | Low impact for SeaNymph | Note only |
| **Electrical breakdown excluded** | Failure of electrical equipment (alternator, batteries, wiring) not covered | Moderate concern — solar/battery upgrades are expensive | Verify scope |
| **Freezing excluded** | Ice damage not covered if boat was not properly winterized | High concern | Document winterization |
| **"Seaworthy condition" clause** | Claim can be denied if insurer argues boat was not in seaworthy condition at time of loss | **Most problematic (2/5 reasonableness)** | See below |
| **Binding arbitration** | Disputes go to private arbitration instead of court | Limits ability to sue insurer | Understand terms |

---

## The "Seaworthy Condition" Clause — Most Important Risk

This clause is the most dangerous because it is vague. "Seaworthy condition" is undefined in most policies, giving the insurer latitude to deny almost any claim by arguing the boat was in some way deficient.

**Examples of how it could be invoked:**
- A seacock that had been leaking slowly before the incident
- An outdated life jacket count
- Faulty wiring that contributed to an electrical fire
- A standing rigging issue that predated the incident

**Defense:** Maintain a paper trail of ongoing maintenance. Annual haul-out records, seacock inspection notes, rigging inspection receipts, and oil change documentation all argue against a "not seaworthy" claim denial.

## Professional Winterization — Now a Documentation Requirement

The freezing exclusion has made professional winterization documentation practically essential. If a pipe cracks or the raw water pump freezes and bursts, and the insurer asks "prove it was properly winterized," a signed work order from a boatyard is the strongest possible answer.

**What counts:**
- Signed receipt from a Maine boatyard showing winterization service performed, dated, and describing work done (antifreeze through cooling system is the critical item)
- Even if you do most of the work yourself, having a yard antifreeze the engine and sign off on it creates a defensible record

**What doesn't count:**
- Your own notes or memory
- A receipt for antifreeze purchased at a hardware store with no other documentation

See [[winterizing]] for the full procedure.

## Policy Types — Agreed Value vs. Actual Cash Value

**Agreed Value (AV):** The insurer pays the agreed amount if the boat is a total loss. Negotiated at policy inception. Best choice for a maintained older boat — avoids the insurer arguing depreciation.

**Actual Cash Value (ACV):** The insurer pays depreciated value. A 1983 CD25D that you've invested $20,000 into might have an ACV of $15,000 — the market value of a comparable un-upgraded boat. You lose the improvement value.

> Insist on Agreed Value. If your insurer only offers ACV, shop elsewhere.

## Annual Checklist — Maintain Documentation

- [ ] Winterization receipt from boatyard (or self-documented record)
- [ ] Engine oil change record (date, mileage/hours)
- [ ] Rigging inspection notes (annual)
- [ ] Seacock inspection record (annual haul-out)
- [ ] Fire extinguisher service date
- [ ] Life jacket count and condition
- [ ] Flare kit expiration dates

Keep these records together — a small folder in the boat or a digital photo archive. If you ever need to make a claim, these are what stand between a paid claim and a denied one.
