# Mast Climbing — Bosun's Chair and Alternatives

Going aloft on the CD25D is required for inspecting and replacing masthead equipment (VHF antenna, wind instruments, sheaves, light bulbs, anchor light), addressing rig problems, and annual rigging inspection. The mast is tall enough that a fall is potentially fatal. Every method below requires careful planning and execution.

> **Singlehanding warning:** A solo aloft climb is dangerous. If you fall or become stuck, there is no one on deck. The only safe solo alternative is to lower the mast. For any aloft work with crew, a dedicated helper on deck is mandatory throughout the climb.

---

## Method 1: Bosun's Chair (Traditional)

The most common approach for infrequent aloft work. A bosun's chair is a wood or canvas seat attached to a halyard.

### Equipment Needed

- **Bosun's chair** — a rigid seat (wood plank with canvas sides) or a fabric harness-style chair
- **Halyard:** Use the spinnaker halyard or main halyard — whichever is the most direct run to the masthead and can be tailed to a winch. Use a masthead-sheaved halyard (not a mid-mast exit) for full-height access.
- **Safety line:** A second halyard or line tailing around the mast, tied to the chair with a slip knot so it can be advanced as you climb. This prevents a freefall if the primary halyard fails.
- **Halyards for the chair:** ½" nylon braid is traditional. Use a bowline to attach the chair; double-check the knot before leaving the deck.

### Procedure

1. Inspect the halyard: no chafe, no damaged strands near the shackle or masthead sheave. If in doubt, use a different halyard.
2. Attach the bosun's chair to the halyard with a bowline (not a shackle alone — a shackle can unscrew). Tie the safety line to the chair using a slip knot so it can be advanced as you rise.
3. Take up slack on a winch while a helper tails the line. The person going aloft should hold the mast to avoid spinning.
4. Have the helper tail and cleat off after each increment of height. The safety line person advances the safety line after each stop.
5. At the masthead, lash yourself to the mast with a short line through the bosun's chair before doing any work. Don't trust the halyard alone if both hands are occupied.
6. To descend: winch the person up a few inches to take the load off the cleat; ease slowly while the person holds onto the mast to control spin.

### Key Safety Rules

- **Never leave the person aloft without the safety line advanced to their current position.** If the halyard jams or the winch slips, the safety line is the only thing preventing a fall.
- **Never go aloft in more than 5–8 knots of wind.** The mast amplifies motion. In any swell, the masthead describes a significant arc even when the boat feels stable below.
- **Wear a helmet.** A dropped shackle from the masthead hits the deck at lethal velocity.
- Drain pockets and wear gloves.

## Method 2: Mast Climbing Devices

Mechanical climbing devices allow a person to ascend solo, using leg and upper body alternately. The person essentially climbs the mast using the devices as moving anchors. Two devices are purpose-built for sailing:

**ATN Top Climber** — A commercial mast climber specifically designed for sailboats. Two gripping clamps that alternately grip the halyard; the user steps up in a motion similar to ascending a rope. Designed to work with a standard halyard and existing sail tracks.

**SJS PrimeClimb** — Another purpose-built mast climber with a similar ascending mechanism. Used by single-handed ocean racers for self-rescue at sea.

**Rock climbing ascenders** (Jumar style) — Two mechanical devices that grip the rope when loaded and slide freely when unloaded. Highly reliable and familiar to anyone with climbing experience. Require a static or semi-static line (not dynamic climbing rope) — a halyard works. This approach is the most adaptable if you already have climbing gear.

### Using Ascenders

1. Rig a static line (halyard) from the masthead, tail on deck.
2. Attach a foot loop to the lower ascender and a chest harness connection to the upper.
3. Ascend by standing on the lower ascender while sliding the upper one up; then sit/hang on the upper while sliding the lower one up. Repeat.
4. Always have a backup line attached at chest level.

### Mast Track Consideration

On the CD25D, the main halyard runs close to the mast track. ATN Top Climber is designed to work with the track present; ascenders may need to be rigged on the spinnaker or genoa halyard to get a clear run.

## Method 3: Lower the Mast

The CD25D's mast is tabernacle-mounted (the mast partners allow it to be stepped and unstepped). Lowering the mast entirely eliminates the risk of working at height. It is the correct approach for major work: replacing masthead instruments, inspecting sheaves, or any task that requires more than a few minutes of hands-on work.

**When to lower the mast:**
- Installing or replacing the anchor light, VHF antenna, wind instrument
- Full rigging inspection
- Any work that would take more than 5 minutes at the masthead
- If working alone

**Lowering requires:** a boom or A-frame to support the mast as it comes down; a helper or two. On the CD25D, the mast is heavy — this is not a solo operation.

> **Note:** Many Maine yards (Rockland area) will lower and raise a mast for a reasonable hourly fee. For occasional work, professional mast-up/down service may be more practical than rigging a solo system.

---

## Method 4: Hire a Rigger

For inspections and equipment replacement that happen infrequently, a professional rigger is the practical answer. Maine riggers are accustomed to single-spreader masthead rigs and charge several hundred dollars per aloft visit — less than the cost of a fall.

**When to hire:**
- Standing rigging inspection (riggers know what to look for)
- Terminal inspection (look for wire separation inside swaged fittings)
- Any work requiring two hands with no way to secure yourself independently

## Singlehanding Notes

Going aloft single-handed is not recommended for any method that requires someone on deck handling halyards. The options available to a solo operator:

1. **Mast climbers (ATN/SJS/ascenders):** The person ascending controls their own progress. No one needed on deck. This is the intended solo application. Still requires anchoring out in calm conditions — the boat must be stationary and in no danger.
2. **Lower the mast:** Involves a rigger or helper, but the work itself is done at deck level.
3. **Wait and recruit help.** For most aloft tasks, waiting until a crew member or marina helper is available is the lowest-risk option.

If you must go aloft alone on ascenders: anchor in a protected, calm location. Leave a VHF channel 16 radio in your pocket. Brief the marina on the VHF before ascending. Have a knife accessible to cut yourself free if you become tangled.
