# Seacocks — Cape Dory 25D

Seacocks are the valves controlling all through-hull fittings below the waterline. They are the boat's most safety-critical components — a failed seacock can sink the boat. On a 40-year-old Cape Dory, inspection and likely replacement is essential.

## The Five Seacocks

A standard-build CD25D has **five below-waterline seacocks**:

| System | Size (NPS) | Hose ID | Location | Access |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engine raw water intake | 3/4" | 3/4" | Starboard, engine compartment | Relatively easy |
| Galley sink drain | 3/4" | 3/4" | Port, near companionway ladder | Difficult — cramped |
| Head intake | 3/4" | 3/4" | Forward, head compartment | Generally accessible |
| Head discharge | 1-1/2" | 1-1/2" | Forward, head compartment | Generally accessible |
| Cockpit scuppers (x2) | 1-1/2" | 1-5/8" | Port & starboard, engine compartment / aft lockers | Starboard: manageable. **Port: extremely difficult.** |

> **Note:** Engine exhaust and bilge pump discharge exit through the transom above/near the waterline and have no factory seacocks.

## Original Hardware

Factory seacocks were **Spartan Marine all-bronze tapered cone** seacocks — considered top-of-the-line. After ~40 years, replacement is prudent regardless of apparent condition. The original plywood backing plates are almost certainly waterlogged and degraded.

## Access Challenges — The Hull Liner Problem

The CD25D was built with a **full molded fiberglass hull liner** that incorporates the furniture. Systems were run before the liner was installed and fiberglassed in. This creates permanent inaccessible voids between the liner and hull, especially on the port side.

**Port-side seacocks are genuinely very hard to reach.** This is not a fixable problem — it's the nature of the construction:

- The **port cockpit drain** is the worst. One owner spent three hours attempting to reach it and found it impossible from the cockpit locker, eventually accessing it from behind the removed companionway stairs. Others describe needing "the acrobatics of a tiny gymnast."
- The **galley sink drain** (also port) is difficult but slightly better — removing the companionway ladder and engine access panel helps.
- **Starboard seacocks** are considerably more accessible.

**Planning implication:** Budget extra time and patience for the port side. Have the companionway ladder, engine panels, and any other removable interior furniture out before starting.

## Seacock vs. In-Line Ball Valve — Critical Distinction

A proper **seacock** has a wide flange that is through-bolted directly to the hull (3–4 bolts). This makes it a structural part of the hull.

An **in-line ball valve** (often mistakenly called a seacock) threads onto the through-hull shank. This is dangerous:
- Creates thread mismatch (through-hulls use NPS straight threads; ball valves use NPT tapered threads)
- Creates a cantilevered load on a thin threaded shank that can snap off under stress
- **Never use an in-line ball valve as a seacock**

Only **UL-Marine rated, flanged, all-bronze seacocks** are acceptable below the waterline.

## Current Status (as of 2026-04-13)

- **Galley sink drain seacock:** New backing plate installed, awaiting final fastening. Nearly complete.
- All other seacocks: status unknown — inspect on next haul-out.

## Singlehanding Implications

- Seacock replacement requires haul-out — plan this as a yard project
- The final installation step (holding the through-hull from outside while threading the seacock inside) is much easier with two people. A singlehander should arrange a helper for at least the installation day, or use a specialized technique (some owners thread monofilament through to hold the through-hull temporarily)
- A singlehander at sea should know the location of every seacock and be able to close all of them quickly — this is a key safety drill
- Keep wooden emergency bungs (one per through-hull, tapered softwood) tied near each seacock
