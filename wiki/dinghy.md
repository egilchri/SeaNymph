# Dinghy — Zodiac, Outboard Motor, and Towing

The dinghy is SeaNymph's tender: an ~8-foot Zodiac inflatable with a 2.5 HP gas outboard. For Penobscot Bay use, the dinghy is the shore link — essential for anchoring out at Pulpit Harbor, Perry Creek, and other anchorages where you can't get to a dock. For a singlehander, every dinghy operation (launching, retrieving, towing, motoring to shore) happens alone.

---

## The Dinghy

An ~8-foot Zodiac inflatable. Key characteristics:
- Soft inflatable floor (not rigid/high-pressure air deck) — will not plane reliably, displacement speeds only (3–5 knots)
- Three bow attachment points: D-ring port, D-ring starboard, center bow handle
- Two stern attachment points on transom for motor mount
- Max rated power: 6–8 HP (Zodiac Cadet class); practical recommendation is 2.5–4 HP for this size

---

## Outboard Motor — Current and Options

### Current Setup: 2.5 HP Gas Outboard

The boat currently has a 2.5 HP 4-stroke outboard. This is appropriate for the 8-foot Zodiac for harbor-to-shore trips at displacement speeds. For reference, a 3.5 HP motor provides noticeably more headway against current without significant weight penalty (Mercury 3.5 and Tohatsu 3.5 share an engine block and weigh the same ~41 lbs as their 2.5 HP siblings).

**Major 2.5–3.5 HP gas 4-stroke options (2025 prices):**

| Model | HP | Price | Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suzuki DF2.5 | 2.5 | $1,120–1,150 | **30 lbs** | Lightest in class; no reverse |
| Mercury 2.5 MH | 2.5 | $900–965 | 38–41 lbs | Most affordable; no reverse |
| Yamaha F2.5 | 2.5 | $1,000–1,175 | 37 lbs | 3-way storage positions |
| Tohatsu MFS2.5 | 2.5 | $1,023–1,089 | 41 lbs | Same block as Mercury 3.5 |
| Mercury 3.5 MH | 3.5 | $1,100–1,150 | 41 lbs | Shared block with Tohatsu 3.5 |
| Tohatsu MFS3.5 | 3.5 | $1,068–1,240 | 41 lbs | F-N-R gearing; recommended step-up |

**Avoid:** Hangkai, Yadao, and other cheap off-brand motors. Mixed reliability in freshwater, poor track record in saltwater. No dealer support for Rockland-area service.

### Why Consider Electric

For SeaNymph specifically, an electric dinghy motor has real appeal:
- **No fuel storage on the sailboat** — eliminates a safety and storage hassle; one less jerry can
- **No fumes in the cockpit** while motoring with dinghy alongside
- **Quiet operation** — Penobscot Bay anchorages at dawn/dusk
- **12V charging from SeaNymph's solar-topped battery bank** is feasible (see below)
- **Simpler maintenance** — no oil changes, no carburetor issues, no winterization

The trade-off is higher upfront cost and limited range per charge.

## Electric Outboard Analysis

### Temo 450 — The Leading Option

The Temo 450 is the most practical electric choice for a cruising sailor with an 8-foot Zodiac. Its defining feature is extreme portability: the motor, battery, and control are a single 11-lb unit. It carries aboard SeaNymph without drama.

**Floats if dropped overboard** — this is unique in the market.

**Temo 450 Carbon:**
- Same motor, carbon fiber body
- Weight: 4.6 kg (slightly lighter)
- Battery: 323 Wh (10% more range)
- Price: ~$2,490
- Warranty: 5 years
- Worth considering if budget allows and you want the longer warranty

**What the Temo does well:**
- Harbor-to-shore trips at anchorage
- Shallow water approach (7" draft)
- Carrying aboard or stowing in a bag easily
- Running off SeaNymph's 12V system via optional charger

**Temo limitations:**
- Not ideal for close-quarters harbor maneuvering — reverse is more of a brake than true reverse
- In swelly conditions, an unusual noise (described as "farting") has been reported by some users
- Range is limited — 40 minutes at full throttle; plan accordingly
- Battery is not user-replaceable in the field

## Charging the Temo from SeaNymph

The Temo 450 can be charged from SeaNymph's 12V battery bank using the optional TEMO 12V charger converter (~$80–100 accessory).

**12V charging specs:**
- Charger draw: 8.3A at 12V (100W)
- Total energy for full charge: 30 Ah from the 12V bank
- Charge time: ~3.5 hours

**Impact on SeaNymph's battery bank:**

30 Ah is meaningful — it's roughly 25% of a Group 24 AGM battery. With the Victron 75/15 MPPT solar system running (see [[solar-system]]), a full day at anchor should comfortably recover that. Charge the Temo during peak solar hours (10am–3pm).

**Best practice at anchor:**
1. Motor to anchorage; arrive with dinghy aboard or in tow
2. Deploy dinghy and Temo for shore run
3. Return to SeaNymph; plug Temo into 12V charger via cockpit socket
4. Solar charges it back in 3–4 hours during the day

**Shore charging:** The standard charger is 110V. Shore power at Knight Marine (when available) is the easiest full-recharge option.

**Service in Rockland:** Ocean Pursuits boatyard and Midcoast Marine Electronics have been identified as potential Temo service contacts. Confirm before relying on either. TEMO US support: +1 (207) 200-5649.

## Towing the Dinghy Behind SeaNymph

For short harbor moves (e.g., sailing to the boatyard), the Zodiac can be towed behind the CD25D.

> **Note:** The CD25D's tow painter is not a floating line. The critical rule is keep the line short and taut to prevent it from sinking and fouling the prop.

### Towing Procedure

**Before departing:**
1. Remove the outboard motor from the Zodiac's transom — lift it aboard SeaNymph and secure in the cockpit or on deck. Towing with motor on causes poor tracking and swamping risk.
2. Remove everything from the Zodiac — oars, fuel tank, anchor, bailer. A light dinghy tows far better.

**Attaching the tow line:**
- Tie to the **center bow handle** or one of the bow D-rings with a bowline
- Do not tie to the backstay, pushpit, or any fitting not designed for load

**Securing to SeaNymph:**
- Use both stern cleats to create a bridle:
  1. Run the tow line to one stern cleat; take one turn (don't cleat off)
  2. Lead across to the second stern cleat; cleat hitch
- This centers the dinghy directly behind the transom rather than sheering to one side

**Tow length — the most critical step:**
- Keep the dinghy **3–5 feet from the transom**
- At this distance, the non-floating line stays short, steep, and taut — completely out of the water
- The Zodiac rides on the bow wave of the CD25D's wake

**Underway:**
- Speed: 2–3 knots — no more
- Make **wide turns** — the dinghy swings out on turns; a sharp turn can capsize it
- Check the tow line periodically; if it goes slack at any point, shorten it immediately

**Final approach to dock:**
- Slow down well before arriving
- Hand-pull the dinghy alongside; hold it by hand or tie it alongside with two short lines for the final approach
- Never try to dock with the dinghy still on the tow line astern

### Towing Checklist

- [ ] Outboard removed from Zodiac; secured on SeaNymph
- [ ] All gear removed from Zodiac
- [ ] Tow line: bowline to Zodiac center bow handle
- [ ] Tow line: bridled to both stern cleats on CD25D
- [ ] Tow length: 3–5 feet from transom (line taut, out of water)
- [ ] Speed: 2–3 knots maximum
- [ ] Dinghy brought alongside for final approach and docking

## Singlehanding Notes

- **Dinghy launching and retrieval**: The most awkward single-handed operation. The Zodiac is inflatable — deflate it for daysails without anchoring; reinflate and re-motor at the anchorage if needed. For longer cruises, keep it inflated and in tow or on deck.
- **Temo portability advantage**: The 11-lb Temo goes from cockpit to dinghy transom by hand — no lifting a heavy gas motor solo over the rail.
- **Shore trips at anchor**: Always secure SeaNymph before leaving in the dinghy — set anchor alarm on the chartplotter (see [[anchoring]]), close hatches. Going ashore solo in the dinghy means no one is aboard.
- **Painter management**: In Penobscot Bay anchorages, a short painter (8–10 ft) when the dinghy is alongside prevents it from swinging under the bow or stern. At night, tie the dinghy off short to a stern cleat so it doesn't bang the hull.
