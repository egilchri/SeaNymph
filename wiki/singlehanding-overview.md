# Singlehanding — Overview and Core Practices

Singlehanding is not simply sailing with less crew. It changes the risk profile of every operation, because there is no one to back you up. The margin between a manageable problem and a catastrophe is thinner alone. The disciplines that matter most — planning, conservatism, gear preparation — are the same ones experienced singlehanders emphasize consistently.

---

## The Three Core Risks

**Man overboard (MOB):** If you go over the side, no one is aboard to turn the boat around. Prevention is the only reliable mitigation — recovery is very unlikely alone. This single fact should shape every decision about working on deck.

**Incapacitation:** A singlehander who is injured, seasick, or incapacitated cannot call for help (unless VHF/EPIRB is within reach) and cannot sail the boat. The boat will drift or sail unattended. Being overdue triggers a search — but that takes time.

**Overload:** A singlehander must helm, navigate, trim, anchor, dock, and handle emergencies simultaneously. Cognitive and physical overload at a critical moment — busy harbor entrance, fog, squall — is the most common proximate cause of singlehanding incidents.

---

## Prevention: MOB

**Clip in before leaving the cockpit.** No exceptions when offshore, in heavy conditions, or at night. The rule applies even for a quick trip to the foredeck.

- Wear a **combination inflatable PFD/harness** with a crotch strap — a harness without a crotch strap can slide off in the water
- Use a **tether with a short (6 ft) and long (8 ft) leg** — short for working on deck; long for moving between cockpit and foredeck
- Rig **jacklines** before departure: runs from cockpit to bow on each side, attached to through-bolted deck eyes. Use flat webbing jacklines — round line rolls underfoot. Keep jacklines inboard of the rail so a fall pulls you onto the deck, not off the side.

**Cockpit safety (where most time is spent):**
- The cockpit coaming is the last thing between you and the water when a wave hits
- Tether to a cockpit cleat or dedicated pad eye, not the traveler or sheet blocks
- Keep the companionway unlocked at all times underway — you need to get below quickly

**At anchor:** The risk drops significantly when anchored. But boarding from the dinghy — especially returning to SeaNymph from shore — is a moment of vulnerability. Come alongside amidships, hold on, and step aboard deliberately.

## Essential Gear for Singlehanding

**Tiller pilot** — the most important piece of electronics. Without one, you cannot leave the helm to handle sails, navigate, or respond to problems. The Raymarine ST2000+ is the planned fit; see [[tiller-pilot]]. Until installed, use a tiller lock or bungee arrangement for very short periods only.

**Harness and tether** — not optional offshore or in deteriorating conditions. See above and [[safety-gear]].

**VHF with DSC and MMSI** — if you go over the side and manage to trigger the Distress button, the Coast Guard gets your position within 3 seconds. Program MMSI before the season. See [[vhf]].

**Anchor alarm** — going below for rest at anchor is only possible with an anchor alarm set. Use the chartplotter's alarm with a tight radius. See [[anchoring]].

**Tidal awareness** — a singlehander who is tired or disoriented and misreads the current can end up in a dangerous situation faster than a crew can collectively assess. Build a tidal awareness habit before any transit in Penobscot Bay. See [[penobscot-bay]].

## Watchkeeping

A singlehander cannot maintain a continuous 24-hour watch. On overnight passages, the only realistic approach is **polyphasic rest** — short sleep periods of 20–30 minutes with an alarm, cycling throughout the night.

**Minimum watchkeeping practice:**
- Set a timer for no more than 20 minutes when resting below
- Set an anchor alarm whenever anchored
- Use AIS to see traffic — but AIS does not show lobster pots, deadheads, or unequipped boats
- In fog, reduce speed to the point where you can stop in half your visibility distance; post a sound watch

**At anchor overnight:**
- Anchor alarm on chartplotter, radius = half the estimated swing circle
- Cockpit hatch cracked for sound — you will hear a dragging anchor before the alarm if you're awake
- Check the anchor bearings before sleep; check again at any wind shift

## Passage Planning

**Conservative itinearies:** A singlehander has less reserve — physical, cognitive, and mechanical — than a crewed boat. Plan passages that leave margin. The Maine coast rewards the boat that leaves early and arrives by noon; it punishes the one chasing a schedule into the afternoon southwesterly.

**Bail-out options:** On any passage, identify harbors of refuge at 10–15 mile intervals. If conditions deteriorate faster than expected, having a clear bail-out harbor already identified means the decision is made before stress sets in.

**Pre-departure checks for singlehanding:**
- [ ] Tiller pilot tested and functional (or backup steering plan in place)
- [ ] Harness and tether rigged and accessible from companionway
- [ ] Jacklines rigged if offshore or if conditions warrant
- [ ] VHF on, channel 16 monitored, MMSI programmed
- [ ] Float plan left with someone ashore (destination, ETA, contact plan)
- [ ] Flares accessible from cockpit
- [ ] Seacocks confirmed open/closed as appropriate

## Reefing Solo

Reef early — before you need to. A singlehander reefing in building conditions on a boat that is already overpowered is dealing with a loaded situation alone. The CD25D's full keel helps (the boat is forgiving), but a flogging mainsail and spray in your face make everything harder.

**Rule of thumb:** If you think you might need a reef, put it in now. It is much easier to shake it out if the wind doesn't materialize than to put it in under pressure.

Singlehanded reefing procedure:
1. Head up to reduce load on the mainsail
2. Ease the mainsheet fully — the sail should just be flogging, not pulling
3. Drop the halyard to the reef point; secure the reefing tack cringle at the gooseneck
4. Tension the halyard
5. Pull the reefing clew line to tension the foot
6. Roll or tie the bunt neatly — loose sail is a noise and chafe problem
7. Re-trim; bear away to course

> **Singlehanding note:** Step away from the helm to reef means the tiller pilot must be engaged first. Confirm the pilot is steering before going forward.

## Docking and Anchoring Solo

These are the highest-workload moments for a singlehander.

**Docking:** Slow down well before the dock. The CD25D's full keel is slow to respond to rudder — plan the approach assuming poor maneuverability. Have lines ready to hand on both sides before the final approach. Never approach a dock faster than you're willing to hit it. See [[rockland-harbor]] for Rockland-specific notes.

**Anchoring:** The most dangerous moment is when the anchor breaks out and you're at the bow with the helm unsecured. Options: (a) neutral gear before going forward, or (b) tiller pilot engaged to hold course. See [[anchoring]] for the full solo procedure.
