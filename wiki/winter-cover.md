# Winter Cover — Mast-Up Storage

SeaNymph winters with the mast up in Rockland. The cover must work around a standing rig: no simple A-frame or flat drape. The goal is a boom-tent system that sheds snow load, allows ventilation, and protects the deck and cabin through a Maine winter.

---

## Framework — The Boom-Tent System

The boom is the ridgepole. Everything else hangs from it.

**Setup:**
1. **Raise the boom** above its normal sailing position by extending the topping lift — 12–18 inches above sailing height is the target. The boom should create a steep ridge, not a flat shelf.
2. **Tension the downhauls** on both sides of the boom to prevent swinging under snow load. The boom should be locked solidly at the ridge height — any swing will chafe the cover against the standing rigging.
3. **Forward ridgeline:** Run a line from the mast (below the masthead anchor point) forward to the bow pulpit or forestay. This pulls the cover's forward section into a tent shape and prevents a "pool" from forming just forward of the mast.
4. **Rafters:** Some owners add athwartship spars from the boom out to the toerails or stanchions to create true tent geometry. PVC pipe or wooden dowels work for small spans. This is valuable if the sail plan is wide — it eliminates the "hammock" effect where the cover sags between the boom and the rail.

## Material Selection

The material decision is the most important long-term choice. Cheap materials fail and can damage the boat in the process.

| Material | Breathability | Durability | Lifespan | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Polyethylene tarp (blue/silver) | None | Poor | 1–2 seasons | Shreds in winter gales; traps moisture; avoid |
| Waxed cotton canvas | High | Good | 5–10 years | Heavy, requires drying before storage; absorbs water if wax fails |
| Polyester canvas (treated) | High | Very good | 8–12 years | Best balance; lighter than cotton; recommended |
| Sunbrella (acrylic canvas) | High | Good | 8–15 years | Moderate abrasion resistance; needs reinforcement at chafe points |
| Top Gun (coated polyester) | Low | Excellent | 10–15+ years | Best for harsh Maine winters; strong, mildew-resistant; needs ventilation gaps |
| Heavy vinyl (18 oz) | None | Excellent | 10–15+ years | Waterproof but no breathability; must engineer ventilation; heavy |

**Recommended:** For a mast-up Penobscot Bay winter, **coated polyester (Top Gun, Odyssey)** or **polyester canvas** hits the sweet spot — tough enough for nor'easters, easier to handle than vinyl. Sunbrella is also excellent if a local canvas shop works in it. Top Gun is the professional marine canvas shop's default for cold climates.

> **Avoid poly tarps entirely.** The sailing community is uniform on this: they shred, trap moisture, and cause the damage they're supposed to prevent. They are acceptable only as a temporary cover for a week, not a winter.

## Economic Analysis

A custom fitted cover for a 25-foot sailboat costs approximately $1,500–2,500. Annual shrinkwrapping runs $700–1,000/year in Maine.

**Break-even:** 2–4 seasons. A custom cover that lasts 10–15 years is a very strong long-term value.

**Semi-custom approach:** Purchase marine-grade polyester canvas (MyTarp.com, TarpsNow.com) and cut/sew to fit with sewn webbing loops and spur grommets. Requires significant effort but saves half the cost. See [[canvas-work]] for hardware guidance.

## Access Door

A cover that can't be opened without removing it entirely is a problem — you'll need access to pump bilge, adjust the cover, or retrieve gear.

**Best option:** A sewn-in zipper using a YKK #10 Vislon zipper. These are designed for marine canvas; they zip reliably when cold and wet. The zipper should be positioned at the companionway hatch so you can board without taking the cover off.

Alternative: An overlapping flap with stainless snap fasteners. Simpler to build, but less weatherproof.

---

## Ventilation

Non-breathable materials require deliberate ventilation engineering. Breathable materials (polyester canvas, Sunbrella) allow moisture to escape and are more forgiving, but still benefit from airflow.

**Target:** 6 vents minimum for a 25-foot boat. More is better.

**Methods:**
- Leave the hem 6–12 inches off the toerail all the way around — creates a circumferential air gap (works well with vinyl or poly)
- Install mushroom vents sewn into the cover fabric (available from Sailrite)
- Use the companionway cowl vent through a slot in the cover
- Prop the forward hatch open with a small block under the cover lip

Without ventilation, condensation accumulates inside and mildew colonizes everything: the teak, the upholstery, the sails in their bags, the wiring.

---

## Chafe Protection — Critical Step

The cover moves in the wind. Any hard point on the boat will abrade through even the toughest fabric given enough time.

**Common chafe points:**
- Stanchion tops (wrap each with foam pipe insulation, secured with tape)
- Winch drums (pad with old towels, carpet scraps)
- Cleats
- Spreader tips (pad spreaders before the cover goes on)
- Any bolt heads, cotter pins, or antenna mounts that protrude above deck

Inspect the cover every 4–6 weeks over winter and pad any new hot spots. A chafe hole in January means water damage through March.

---

## Wind Securement

A loose cover in a nor'easter can generate enough force to rip itself apart or topple the boat on jackstands. It must be lashed drum-tight.

**Techniques:**
- Lash with ½" line from cover grommets to stanchion bases, cleats, or under-hull straps. Run lines athwartships under the keel if the cover extends that far.
- **The fishing net trick:** Drape a large-mesh net (available from commercial fishing suppliers) over the entire cover, then lash the net. The net distributes wind load across the full surface area instead of concentrating it on individual grommets.
- Check lashing tension after the first windstorm; it always needs retightening.

---

## "Tennis Ball Trick" for Poly Tarps

If you end up using a poly tarp short-term: press a tennis ball against the outside of the tarp where you want a tie-down point, wrap bungee cord around the ball, and cleat off the bungee. This creates a tie-down without cutting a hole in the tarp and without a grommet. Not a permanent solution but useful for tarps.

---

## Singlehanding Notes

- Installing or removing a winter cover solo is awkward for a 25-footer. The forward ridgeline can be pre-rigged and then the cover slid over the mast from the stern, but this requires careful planning.
- A cover with sewn webbing loops at regular intervals is much easier to lash solo than one with grommets at the corners only.
- Leave the cover in a way that you can board the boat for mid-winter inspection without a struggle — a zipper at the companionway is essential.
