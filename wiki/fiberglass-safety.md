# Fiberglass and Epoxy Safety

Working with epoxy and fiberglass on the CD25D is unavoidable — seacock backing plates, hole potting, deck repairs. The safety stakes are higher than most maintenance tasks because **epoxy sensitization is permanent and irreversible.** This is the single most important fact in this page.

---

## The Central Risk: Epoxy Sensitization

Epoxy resins (and to a lesser extent, fiberglass dust) can cause an allergic immune response called sensitization. Once sensitized, the immune system treats epoxy as a serious threat. Subsequent exposures — even tiny amounts, even brief skin contact — trigger increasingly severe reactions: contact dermatitis, respiratory reactions, or anaphylaxis in severe cases.

**The critical point: there is no cure and no desensitization. Once you are sensitized, you are sensitized for life.**

You cannot tell if you are sensitizing before you have already done so. The first few exposures may cause no obvious reaction. Sensitization builds silently. Many woodworkers and boatbuilders sensitize during years of casual exposure, then one day discover they can no longer work with epoxy at all.

**This is not hypothetical.** It is well-documented and common in the boatbuilding community.

---

## Respiratory Protection

> **Warning:** A standard dust mask (N95 or paper mask) is NOT adequate for epoxy work. It does not filter organic vapors. You can be fully masked with an N95 and still breathe epoxy vapors at harmful levels.

**Required:** An OV/P100 combination cartridge respirator.

| Product | Notes |
|---|---|
| 3M 60921 (OV/P100) | Standard half-face OV/P100 cartridge pair; fits 3M 6000/7000 series masks |
| 3M 60923 (OV/P100/HMIS P100) | Same protection, wider chemical coverage |

**How to use:**
- Fit-test the mask before use — it must seal to the face. Beards prevent a seal.
- OV cartridges saturate over time. Replace when you begin to smell vapors, or on the manufacturer's schedule (typically every 40–80 hours of use).
- After opening cartridge packaging, the activated carbon begins absorbing vapors from ambient air. Use within 6 months of opening, or per manufacturer guidance.

## Skin Protection

Epoxy can sensitize through skin contact, not just inhalation. The skin protection goal is zero contact.

**Gloves:** Nitrile, 6–8 mil thickness. **Not** latex (epoxy components can pass through latex) and **not** cotton (cotton absorbs epoxy and keeps it in contact with skin).

- Double-glove for heavy work — change outer pair frequently
- Cuff the gloves over the sleeve to prevent resin from running inside
- Inspect gloves before each use; discard if torn or contaminated on the outside

> **Never use solvents (acetone, alcohol, lacquer thinner) to clean epoxy from skin.** Solvents dramatically increase epoxy skin penetration by stripping the skin's protective barrier and carrying epoxy molecules directly into the tissue. Wipe off epoxy with a dry cloth first, then wash thoroughly with soap and water.

**Clothing:** Long sleeves and long pants. Disposable Tyvek coveralls are the professional standard — they prevent skin contact even when you brush against a wet surface or kneel in resin drips.

---

## Eye Protection

Epoxy splatter causes serious eye injury and can sensitize through ocular membranes.

**Required:** ANSI Z87.1 rated chemical splash goggles — specifically rated **D3** (splash-proof) or **D4** (fine dust and splash). Safety glasses with open sides are not adequate for epoxy work.

Standard safety glasses allow droplets to enter from the side or above. Full-coverage goggles with indirect ventilation (preventing direct liquid entry) are the correct choice.

---

## Ventilation

Epoxy solvents and amine curing agents are heavier than air and accumulate at floor level and in low-lying spaces like the bilge.

**Required setup:** Extractive ventilation — exhaust fan at the far end of the workspace, with fresh air supply behind the operator. This creates a flow that carries vapors away from you, not across you.

**Critical:** Never rely on ventilation alone as a substitute for respiratory protection. Ventilation reduces vapor concentration; a cartridge respirator provides backup for the residual concentration.

---

## Fiberglass Dust

Grinding, cutting, and sanding fiberglass generates glass fiber particles. These cause mechanical skin irritation (the itch is from tiny glass splinters) and, more importantly, respiratory damage if inhaled.

- The OV/P100 cartridge (required for epoxy work) also filters fiberglass particles via the P100 component
- Grinding fiberglass generates significant dust — the P100 filter is essential
- Tyvek coveralls prevent fiberglass itch significantly
- Rinse off with cold water after fiberglass work (cold water causes skin pores to contract, hot water opens them and lets fibers in)

---

## PPE Checklist for Epoxy/Fiberglass Work

- [ ] OV/P100 half-face respirator (3M 60921 or 60923)
- [ ] Chemical splash goggles (ANSI Z87.1 D3/D4)
- [ ] Nitrile gloves, 6–8 mil, cuffed over sleeves
- [ ] Long sleeves and pants (or disposable Tyvek coveralls)
- [ ] Exhaust ventilation running throughout work
- [ ] No solvents for skin cleanup — dry wipe + soap and water only

---

## Disposal

- Uncured epoxy is hazardous waste — do not pour down drains or into bilge
- Allow small quantities to fully cure; dispose of solid cured epoxy in regular trash (check local regulations)
- Contaminated nitrile gloves and paper towels: bag and dispose as per local chemical waste rules
