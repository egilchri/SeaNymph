# Lobster Pots — Avoidance and Extrication

Every square inch of navigable Maine water is open to the lobster fishery. In Penobscot Bay the density is extraordinary. This page covers how to read the gear, navigate around it, and free yourself if you foul.

See also [[penobscot-bay]] for general hazard context and [[anchoring]] for lobster gear at anchor.

---

## Understanding the Gear

### Conditions Affect Everything

In **calm water at slack tide**: buoys and warps visible, warps drop straight down. Even if you hit a buoy dead-on, the boat often slips by without fouling.

In **tidal current** — which is most of the time in Maine: the buoy lies **down-current** of the trap, and the warp descends at an angle. If you pass too close on the **up-current side**, the diagonal warp passes under the keel and can catch on the keel, rudder, or propeller even if it is not rotating. **Pass on the down-current side.**

### Single Buoy Configuration

The simplest setup: one buoy connected by warp to the trap on the bottom. In current, the buoy is downstream; the warp angles back upstream and down. Pass downstream of the buoy.

**Reading the buoy type:**
- **Foam buoy with stick protruding from the top:** Pass close to the **stick side** — the stick points toward where to go safely.
- **Molded hardshell buoy (no stick):** Pass on the **rounded end**, not the rope end.

### Two-Buoy Configuration — The Critical Trap

Many areas use two floats: a main buoy plus a **toggle** attached to the warp 20–30 feet up from the bottom. The line between the two buoys **sags below the surface** — invisible, waiting to catch your keel, rudder, or propeller.

> **Never go between the two buoys.** The submerged line between them is the hazard. Pass either:
> - **Downstream of the downstream buoy**, OR
> - **Well up-current of the up-current buoy** (remembering that current may set you down faster than expected)

**Identifying which buoy is which in strong current:**
- The buoy suspending the warp from the bottom will often be **lying on its side** in strong current. If you see a buoy lying on its side, or popping upright as waves pass, that's the primary buoy holding the warp — there may be a toggle downstream of it.
- The toggle is often a small, dark-colored cylinder. In very strong current it may be **partly or entirely submerged** — invisible at the surface.

### In Fog and Dark

The challenge of avoiding buoys is **greater in fog** and **prohibitive in the dark.** Do not motor through lobster grounds at night. If caught in fog, slow down significantly — the slower you go, the less likely a glancing contact becomes a foul, and the more time you have to see buoys.

## Avoidance Technique

1. **Assign a dedicated lookout** for buoys when transiting lobster grounds. Singlehanded: slow down and watch from the helm; consider tacking or making longer detours rather than threading tight gaps.
2. **Watch the current constantly** — observe how buoys are lying to understand where the warps are.
3. **Take the engine out of gear immediately** if you cannot avoid a buoy and are under power. A stopped propeller is far less likely to foul than a turning one.
4. **Before committing to a path between buoys**, identify whether there are two buoys and which way the current is running. Never go between two buoys of the same color/owner.
5. In tight channels (Muscle Ridge, Jericho Bay): reduce speed to bare steerage, assign bow watch, be prepared to stop.

## If You Foul

### Signs You've Fouled
- The buoy begins to **follow the boat** — it's hooked to your keel or rudder
- The boat **slows almost to a stop** and falls beam-to or stern-to the current
- **Engine stops** — propeller has fouled and seized

### Immediate Actions
1. **Reduce sail** — get way off the boat quickly
2. **Do NOT engage the propeller** — a turning prop winds warp tighter and can destroy the shaft seal, cutlass bearing, and propeller
3. **Assess your situation** — are you in open water with room to work, or in a tight channel where you need to anchor or call for help immediately?

### Extrication — Step by Step

**First: wait and see (up to 5 minutes)**
If the warp caught on the keel, rudder, or a stopped propeller, it may come off on its own as the warp goes taut and the boat settles. Let the boat drift; don't fight it.

**Pull warp to cleat — useful trick:**
Pull enough warp to the surface that you can cleat off some slack. Taking the tension off whatever is snagged below makes it significantly easier to disentangle. This often allows the fouled section to work free.

**Dinghy investigation:**
After 5 minutes with no improvement, get in the dinghy — firmly tied to the boat, oars in the dinghy. Investigate from the dinghy with a boat hook. You may be able to see the warp hooked around the rudder or prop and free it with the hook.

**Cut the warp:**
If you can pull the warp to the surface, you can cut it. Be aware: the lobsterman loses $200–300 worth of gear and future catch from abandoned traps. This is a last resort, not a first response. After cutting, you still need to remove the remaining warp from the keel, rudder, or prop.

**Call nearby lobstermen:**
If you see lobster boats working nearby, hail them on VHF (channel 16) or by waving. Lobstermen may come to your aid — not just out of kindness but to prevent the loss of their fellow fisherman's gear. They can often free you and save the gear simultaneously.

**Swim under the boat:**
The option of last resort before calling for professional help. Critical considerations:
- Water temperature in Maine: 50–60°F in summer — wetsuit is mandatory, not optional
- Tether yourself to the boat **forward of the boarding ladder** before entering the water
- Stop the engine completely
- You need a mask to see what's fouled; flippers to work effectively
- Consider current: can you hold position while working? When does the tide turn?
- Have a plan for getting back aboard — a boarding ladder that deploys from above water level
- Have crew/plan for controlling the boat while you are in the water

### After Clearing the Fouled Warp
- Inspect the propeller, shaft, and cutlass bearing before motoring
- Shaft-line burns into the cutlass bearing are possible if the warp was wound tight under tension — if bearing feels rough or noisy underway, have it inspected at the next haul-out
- Note the position of cut gear on your chart if possible; consider contacting Maine Marine Patrol or the harbormaster to report lost gear location

## Equipment to Keep Ready

These should be accessible within seconds, not buried in a locker:

| Item | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Knife in sheath on stern pulpit or backstay** | Cut warp at the surface | Inexpensive fisherman's knife; keep it sharp; mount where you can reach it while in the dinghy |
| **Telescoping boat hook + knife taped on** | Cut warp from deck | Duct tape a knife blade to the end of the boat hook to cut a warp without getting in the dinghy |
| **Fiskars P973 anvil ratchet pruner** | Cut line off the propeller | Ratchet action handles heavy braided warp that a knife struggles with |
| **Shaft Shark** (or similar shaft cutter) | Prevent fouling | A cutting blade mounted on the propeller shaft that severs any warp before it wraps. Very effective. |

The **Shaft Shark** is the most preventive option — it cuts the warp before it winds on the prop. Worth installing if lobster pot density in your cruising area warrants it.

## At Anchor — Morning Protocol

Before getting underway from an anchorage where lobster gear was present:

1. **Walk around the boat** — check carefully under the counter and around the rudder
2. **Get in the dinghy** if any doubt — look under the hull
3. **Note the location** of any nearby buoys the night before — write a specific note on the binnacle: *"Find the green/orange buoy off the port quarter"*
4. Don't let the morning routine rush you past this check — fouling your prop 30 feet from the anchorage before you're fully awake is a bad start

## Singlehanding Notes

- Solo avoidance is harder — no dedicated bow watch. Compensate by going slower in lobster grounds and making wider clearances around buoys.
- If you foul while singlehanded: anchor immediately if you're in confined water. Don't attempt to drift while troubleshooting with no one at the helm.
- Swimming under the boat alone is dangerous — if you're incapacitated in the water, no one can help. In Maine water temperatures, you have limited time before cold water affects judgment and motor function. Call for help before attempting to swim.
