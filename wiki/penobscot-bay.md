# Penobscot Bay — Sailing Conditions and Cruising

Home waters for SeaNymph. Penobscot Bay is approximately 40 miles long and 30 miles wide at its mouth, with around 1,000 miles of shoreline and 624 islands and ledges. It is the second-largest embayment on the US East Coast after Chesapeake Bay. Maximum depth approximately 205 feet; deep underwater canyon southeast of Rockland in the West Passage (Penobscot River's ancient channel) exceeds 500 feet.

It's super fun.

## Charts

Primary chart: **NOAA 13302** — Penobscot Bay and Approaches. Always carry up-to-date paper charts as backup. The "Taft Guide" (A Cruising Guide to the Maine Coast by Hank and Jan Taft and Curtis Rindlaub) is the authoritative cruising guide for these waters — widely recommended.

## Sailing Conditions

### Wind

- Late July prevailing: **southwesterly, 5–15 knots** — generally favorable for eastward passages
- Outside the bays: W-NW gusts to 30 knots, seas 2–5 feet possible
- Inside the bays: gusts mostly below 25 knots, seas 1–3 feet
- Local topography and islands create funneling effects — don't assume consistent wind throughout
- Eggemoggin Reach: the SW breeze bends around its southern end; an eastbound vessel may end up hard on the wind for the final stretch

### Fog

Fog is the most critical weather variable on the Maine coast. July and August have the highest incidence of low visibility. The mechanism: warm moist air from the south passes over cold Gulf of Maine water — advection fog. Typically thickest in early morning, may lift by afternoon. Do not count on it.

**Singlehanding in fog:**
- Radar is invaluable — nearly mandatory for reliable fog navigation
- Know COLREGS sound signals before you need them
- Have paper charts aboard; electronic chartplotters can lose power or GPS lock
- Plan a "fog day" contingency on any multi-day cruise — a fogged-in day in Pulpit Harbor is not a failed day

### Tides and Currents

Semi-diurnal tides: two highs and two lows per 24 hours. Tidal range averages 8–10 feet; can reach 18 feet in parts of the bay. These large volumes of moving water generate strong tidal currents, especially in narrow channels and passages.

**Tidal planning is not optional** on a multi-day cruise — timing departures and transits with tidal currents can mean the difference between a manageable passage and a slog against 2+ knots of current.

Notable tidal current stations:
- Fox Islands Thorofare: flood ~0.25kt (070°T), ebb ~0.55kt (278°T) — modest
- Casco Passage: flood ~0.5kt (086°T), ebb ~0.6kt (284°T) — more significant; time this transit

### Navigation Hazards

**Lobster pots:** Millions of traps are set in Maine waters. The warp (line from buoy to trap) can foul a propeller — a serious emergency in confined waters or fog. Precautions:
- Vigilant bow watch at all times
- Denser near harbors, along channel edges, known lobster grounds
- In Jericho Bay: tidal currents run north-south across an easterly course, causing pot toggles to stream out horizontally — hard to see. Treat Jericho Bay as a threading exercise, not autopilot sailing.

**Rocky ledges:** Maine coast is famously rocky. Ledges are well-charted but demand attention. Cross-reference paper charts, chartplotter, and visual observation. Aids to navigation are generally reliable but budget cuts have reduced their numbers in some areas — do not depend solely on any single ATON.

**Anchor note:** Rockland Harbor has a sticky mud bottom — good holding for mushroom anchors and granite blocks. Common in many Penobscot Bay anchorages.

## Key Anchorages and Destinations

### In the Bay

| Location | Distance from Rockland | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Pulpit Harbor, North Haven | 15–20nm | Quintessential Maine anchorage. Excellent protection all directions. Good holding. Spectacular sunsets over Camden Hills. Quiet, residential — no facilities. Enter north of Pulpit Rock. |
| Perry Creek, Vinalhaven | 15–20nm | Protected, good holding in mud. Excellent hiking via Vinalhaven Land Trust trails. McLeod orange-ball moorings available by permission + VLT donation. |
| Fox Islands Thorofare | 15–20nm | Between North Haven and Vinalhaven. Picturesque passage, modest currents. |

### East of the Bay

| Location | Character |
|---|---|
| Eggemoggin Reach | Smooth water, stunning scenery, classic wooden boats, often fog-free, fewer lobster pots than bay. Classic SW reach sailing. |
| Southwest Harbor (MDI) | Full services. Dysart's Great Harbor Marina, Hinckley Yachts. End of a Rockland-to-MDI cruise. |

### Notable Points

- Camden: Picturesque harbor, windjammers, Camden Hills backdrop
- Castine: Rich maritime and Revolutionary War history
- Stonington: Authentic fishing town, artists
- Vinalhaven and North Haven: Island communities, accessible only by boat or ferry
- Isle au Haut: Remote, Acadia National Park land
- Matinicus: Outermost island of the bay — serious offshore sailing

### Lighthouses

Penobscot Bay has more than 15 lighthouses. Notable ones:
- Owls Head Light (south of Rockland)
- Bass Harbor Head Light (MDI, western approach) — one of Maine's most photographed
- Two Bush Light (Muscle Ridge Channel, to south)

## Rockland to Southwest Harbor — Passage Overview

A 2–3 day cruise in late July. Two routes from Rockland toward North Haven:
1. **Muscle Ridge Channel** — sheltered, between mainland and islands; strong currents and numerous ledges demand careful navigation
2. **West Penobscot Bay** — more exposed, faster in favorable conditions

### Two-Day Itinerary
- **Day 1:** Rockland → Pulpit Harbor, North Haven (~15–20nm). Good first stop.
- **Day 2:** Pulpit Harbor → Southwest Harbor (~20–25nm) via Fox Islands Thorofare → Jericho Bay → Casco Passage → Western Way. Long and navigationally demanding but beautiful.

### Three-Day Itinerary
- **Day 1:** Rockland → Perry Creek, Vinalhaven (~15–20nm)
- **Day 2:** Perry Creek → Eggemoggin Reach (~10–15nm, shorter day, explore the Reach)
- **Day 3:** Eggemoggin Reach → Southwest Harbor

## Geology — Sailing Relevance

The bay was carved by glaciers and the islands are former mountain peaks. Rocky ledges are the glacially exposed bedrock. The sticky mud bottom in anchorages is glacial and marine sediment — excellent holding. The deep canyon SE of Rockland explains why the harbor has deep water close in.

## Cruising Season

Late June through early September. Peak season: July–August (also peak fog). Fall sailing (September, early October) often has the best wind and clearest visibility — worth considering.
